What actually changed — and the three things I got wrong

One claim, from one podcast. Everything below is copied from real files. Where I claimed something and the data later contradicted me, that is marked as such rather than quietly dropped.

1 — The input

The test passage

All four runs below read the same 20 minutes of the Jon Stewart transcript (t:5700–6900, 19,183 characters), chosen because it contains a claim that is four people removed from anyone real.

01:42:03  What he did was forwarded me an email that was sent to him from someone he knew inside the DIA.
01:42:11  He's obviously an old, older person in his 70s, not technically savvy.
          …
01:45:29  He said it was a clone extraterrestrial biological entity type two from the Tau Ceti star system.

The chain is: an anonymous person claiming DIA affiliation → an email → an intermediary in his 70s → Jon Stewart → the podcast. Nothing in it is a fact. The only fact is that someone asserted it.

3 — The four runs

Same 20 minutes, two architectures, two models

"Chunked" = the record is cut into ~20,000-character pieces and the model works through them one at a time. "Whole-record" = the model is handed the entire 20 minutes at once and asked to keep going until it says it is finished.

Read the last two columns together. The whole-record architecture found fewer claims and cost more. Opus dropped from 78 claims to 72 and its bill went from $1.12 to $2.69. That is the opposite of what I predicted.

But it did buy one genuinely new thing: a source list

The whole-record pass resolves every source in the record once, up front. Opus found 18 sources, 7 of them distinct anonymous people — and told them apart:

SRC3: a man in his 70s known in ufology, claims knowledge of but not
      membership in classified programmes   via monthly emails to Jon Stewart
SRC4: a person claiming to work inside the Defense Intelligence Agency,
      whose email about the 1991 film was forwarded on
      via [an email to SRC3, SRC3 forwarding it to Jon Stewart]
SRC5: an emailer who asserted the green box detects grey-being vibrations
SRC6: an emailer who asserted Bell Labs built the monitor

Each claim then cites a source by id, and a deterministic step expands it back to the full chain — so every claim from the DIA source gets a byte-identical description. That is what stops one rumour being counted as several. Haiku is much weaker here: 2 anonymous sources against opus's 7, and it blurred the DIA source into a generic one.

4 — The caching, explained properly

Why it didn't work — you were right to query it

What caching is: a discount for sending the same text again. The first send costs 1.25× normal. Every re-send of identical text costs 0.1× — a 90% discount. It only ever pays off if you send the same thing more than once.

The old design

call 1 → piece 1 of the transcript
call 2 → piece 2  (different text!)
call 3 → piece 3  (different again)

Every call is new text, so nothing is ever a re-send. It paid the 1.25× premium over and over and never once collected the discount. Measured: 390k charged at the premium, 139k at the discount.

My fix — and why it failed

call 1 → the whole record
        model replies: "extraction_complete"
        … so there is no call 2

Yes, the loop asks again until the model says it's finished — but it said finished on the first try. One call means no re-send, so no discount. Haiku's discounted tokens: zero.

And the same premature "I'm finished" is why recall dropped

It is one bug, not two. The model reads the whole record, extracts 72 claims, declares itself done, and exits — where the chunked version was forced through every part of the record and found 78. The early exit costs us the claims and the caching at the same time. Fixing that one behaviour would recover both.

5 — Where I was wrong

Three claims I made that the data killed

  1. "Deduplication threw away the good claim." Said it twice, with confidence. Both times wrong. The model had extracted the right passage all along; it just failed to record who said it. I built a causal story on a corrupted location field.
  2. "The same film fragments into several nodes and breaks merging." Wrong. Checking the node types shows it is a document node (the film) and an event node (the filming of it). Those are different things and both should exist. There was no fragmentation bug. I argued for a whole architecture on the back of it.
  3. "Whole-record caching saves 3.2×." Wrong. It cost more — opus went from $1.12 to $2.69. My arithmetic assumed ~15 calls sharing one cached prefix; the redesign collapsed it to one call, so there was nothing to discount.

And one you caught

I labelled sources S1, S2, S3, S4. "S4" is a real place in this transcript — the alleged facility at Papoose Lake — and it appears 87 times. Haiku even wrote "S4" inside a source description. The labels are now SRC1…, with an explicit warning in the prompt never to reuse a name from the record as an id.

6 — So have we improved anything?

Honest assessment

Yes — four things, and they are real

  • The attribution is fixed. The claim that was stored as fact is now stored as an anonymous third-hand assertion, with the chain, and the hedge inside the published sentence. Every model does this, haiku included. This was the actual problem.
  • A live bug was found and killed. Corroboration counted ten podcasts relaying one anonymous email as ten independent confirmations. It was in the code; it hadn't run yet only by luck.
  • Timestamps. 42% of claims recorded their position on the wrong axis (raw seconds, even transcript line numbers). They are now recovered deterministically from the verbatim quote, no model involved. 96% resolve.
  • The source list. Genuinely new, and the one thing the rewrite bought: seven distinct anonymous sources told apart, each with its own chain, described once and cited by id.

Not yet — and one thing got worse

  • Recall. Haiku finds roughly 65% of the claims opus does. That is the real question for picking a model, and it is unchanged by any of this.
  • The whole-record claims pass is worse than the chunked one — fewer claims, higher cost — because it declares itself finished too early. The fix is to keep the new source list (which works) and put the claims pass back on chunks (which covered the record properly).
  • Was I confused from the beginning? Partly, yes. The provenance problem was real and I found it. But I then invented two mechanisms for it that didn't exist, and an architecture to solve a fragmentation bug that wasn't there. What kept catching me was measurement, never reasoning — and that is the strongest argument for not letting me grade my own work.
